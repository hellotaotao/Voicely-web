How to tell whether a transcription app uploads your audio

Many transcription apps on the App Store look alike: a record button, a transcript, an AI summary. Some do the work on your iPhone; others send every recording to a server, and the marketing rarely says which. These four checks tell you in about five minutes, without taking anyone's word for it.

Published November 12, 2026. Examples quoted from the apps' privacy policies on that date.

1. The airplane mode test

Turn on airplane mode and switch Wi-Fi off too, since airplane mode can leave it on. Open the app, record 30 seconds, and stop.

  • If the transcript appears, speech recognition runs on your device.
  • If the app shows an error, waits for a connection, or keeps saying "processing", it transcribes on a server.

This is the only check that does not rely on what the developer says. Passing it proves the transcription is local, not that the app never uploads anything later; the next two checks cover that.

2. The App Store privacy label

Scroll to App Privacy on the app's App Store page. Developers fill it in themselves, and Apple requires it to be accurate.

  • Data Not Collected: the developer says no data leaves the app to them or their partners.
  • Data Linked to You: what they collect is tied to your account or identity.
  • Data Used to Track You: data is combined with other companies' data for advertising, or shared with data brokers.

Every cloud transcription app in our comparison carries "Data Used to Track You". That label covers tracking and ads rather than your recordings, but it tells you how the business works.

3. The privacy policy's list of providers

Search the privacy policy for "transcription", "subprocessor" or "third party". Cloud apps often name the speech company that actually processes your audio. From current policies:

  • Wave: recordings are "uploaded to our server for transcription", transcribed by AssemblyAI, and stored on Google Cloud until you delete them.
  • Minutes: transcription by AssemblyAI, Groq and Deepgram.
  • Summary: transcription by Gladia, Soniox and Replicate.
  • Otter: trains its own AI on de-identified recordings and on transcripts.

Then look for how long recordings are kept and whether they are used for training. "As long as necessary" is the most common answer on retention, and it is not a number.

4. How the app charges

A server-side transcript costs the developer money for every minute of audio, so cloud apps meter it: Otter's free plan gives 300 minutes a month, Notta's free plan transcribes 3 minutes per recording. Weekly subscriptions, such as $8.99 a week on Summary, are common in this category.

An app that runs the model on your device pays nothing extra when you record another hour, so on-device apps are usually a one-time purchase or free, with no minute limits. Pricing alone proves nothing, but a minute quota is a strong hint that a server is involved.

What the checks can't tell you

All four checks describe the app as it is today. An update can change how it works, and the label and policy should change with it. For recordings that are truly sensitive, such as an interview with a source, a medical appointment or a client meeting, repeat the airplane mode test after big updates.
